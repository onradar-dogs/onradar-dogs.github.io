Dachshund Common Health Problems Every Loving Owner Should Know

IVDD affects about 1 in 4 dachshunds. Learn the warning signs, how back disease is graded and treated, which DNA tests matter, and the daily habits that keep your dog healthy.

    If you share your sofa with a long-bodied little hound, learning the dachshund common health problems is one of the kindest things you can do for them. Most dachshunds live long, happy lives — VCA Animal Hospitals gives a typical lifespan of 12–14 years — but a handful of conditions show up again and again in this breed. Knowing them early means you can spot trouble sooner, ask breeders the right questions and make small daily choices that protect the dog you love.

    Quick answer

    The most common serious health problem in dachshunds is intervertebral disc disease (IVDD), which affects roughly 1 in 4 dogs of the breed at some point. Other key concerns are weight gain, inherited Lafora disease (a severe epilepsy mainly in Miniature Wirehaired dachshunds) and cord1 PRA (an inherited eye disease, mainly in Miniature Longhaired dachshunds). Keeping your dog lean, avoiding repeated jumping, choosing DNA-tested parents and acting fast on back pain make the biggest difference.

    Why Are Dachshunds Prone to Back Problems?

    Dachshunds are a chondrodystrophic breed: the same genes that give them short legs also make their spinal discs age early, harden and sometimes burst or bulge into the spinal cord. That is IVDD, and it is by far the most important health issue in the breed.

    A Vet Times review of disc disease genetics explains that in these breeds the gel-like center of each disc is replaced by cartilage-like tissue that later calcifies, typically before one year of age. Researchers have linked an FGF4 retrogene on chromosome 12 to both the short limbs and the disc disease — which is why you cannot breed the long, low dachshund look without accepting some back risk.

    The UK breed’s health organization estimates that about 1 in 4 dachshunds will suffer some degree of back disease during their lifetime, a rate around 10–12 times higher than in the general dog population. In its 2015 survey of more than 16,000 dogs, 17% of owners reported a back problem so far, and most cases were diagnosed between the ages of 4 and 8.

    Does coat type change the risk?

    It seems to. In the same 2015 survey, smooth dachshunds had the highest reported prevalence (25%) and wirehaired dachshunds the lowest (9%). There was no difference between males and females. Coat is not a guarantee either way — a wirehaired dog can still slip a disc — but it is useful context when you are choosing a puppy or planning how careful to be with stairs and furniture.

    Can IVDD come back?

    Yes. Of the 342 affected dogs in that survey, 104 — roughly 3 in 10 — had more than one episode. A dog that has recovered once deserves the same back-friendly routine for life, not just during recovery.     What Are the Signs of IVDD in a Dachshund?

    The early signs are pain and wobbliness; the late sign is paralysis. A dog in pain may yelp when picked up, arch its back, hold its head low or suddenly refuse to jump or climb.

    According to the symptom guidance from Dachshund Health UK, signs fall into three groups:

    • Pain — yelping (unprovoked or when handled), arching of the back, reluctance to jump or climb.
    • Incoordination — obvious stumbling, swaying and wobbliness, most often in the back legs.
    • Paralysis — usually the back legs, sometimes all four, often after a period of incoordination and sometimes with loss of bladder control.

    Because many dachshunds are stoic and food-motivated, a dog can still eat and wag its tail while in real pain. Trust a change in how your dog moves more than how cheerful it looks.

    How Is IVDD Graded and Treated?

    Vets grade IVDD from 1 (painful but walking normally) to 5 (paralyzed with no deep pain sensation in the toes). Mild cases are often managed with strict rest; severe cases are more likely to need surgery.

    The table below summarizes the clinical IVDD grading scale used by Dachshund Health UK, including the outcome ranges it reports. Some clinics use a different or reversed scale, so ask your vet which one they mean.

    What does conservative treatment involve?

    Conservative care means strict confinement, pain relief and, later, physiotherapy. The PDSA notes that strict rest for several weeks is essential for most IVDD cases, and that surgery for severe cases can be very successful when done within a few days of symptoms starting. Strict rest really is strict: a crate or small pen, carried outside for toilet breaks, no sofa, no stairs, no zoomies — even when your dog starts to feel better.

    Why insurance matters for this breed

    Spinal surgery and rehabilitation are among the most expensive treatments a small dog can need, and the PDSA suggests considering insurance early, before any illness begins.     Which Inherited Dachshund Diseases Can a DNA Test Catch?

    Two serious inherited conditions in dachshunds have reliable DNA tests: Lafora disease and cord1 progressive retinal atrophy. Both are recessive, so a dog needs two copies of the faulty gene to be at risk, and testing the parents can prevent affected puppies.

    Lafora disease (mainly Miniature Wirehaired dachshunds)

    Lafora disease is a late-onset inherited epilepsy and one of the most severe forms of canine epilepsy, leading to neurodegeneration. A UK study summarized by Fitzpatrick Referrals tested 733 Miniature Wirehaired dachshunds: 7.0% carried two copies of the mutation and 31.9% were carriers. The good news is that testing works — between 2012 and 2017 the share of at-risk dogs fell from 10.4% to 2.7%, and mutation-free dogs rose from 48.1% to 71.6%.

    cord1 PRA (mainly Miniature Longhaired dachshunds)

    Cone-rod dystrophy 1, a form of progressive retinal atrophy, causes gradual vision loss and eventually blindness. Wisdom Panel’s condition page links it to a variant in the RPGRIP1 gene with autosomal recessive inheritance, and notes that signs can appear anywhere from about 6 months to 10 years of age. Dogs that lose their sight slowly often adapt well if the furniture stays where it is and stairs and pools are blocked off.

    Can you screen for back disease too?

    Partly. The UK runs an IVDD X-ray screening scheme for dogs aged 24 to 48 months. Specialists count calcified discs and grade the result from 0 (none) to 3 (five or more). Dogs with many calcified discs at a young age have a higher risk of later disc problems, and the trait is relatively heritable, so breeders use the score to choose lower-risk pairings.     Why Does Weight Matter So Much for Dachshunds?

    Extra weight loads a spine that is already at risk, and dachshunds gain it easily. VCA notes that dachshunds have a tendency to become obese and that, because small dogs need little food, snacks add excess calories quickly.

    The PDSA also lists keeping a healthy weight as a way to reduce pressure on the spine. Dachshund Health UK’s lifestyle advice is to keep your dog at an ideal body condition — not too lean, not overweight — and to build up a mix of on- and off-lead exercise as a puppy grows. In practice that means you should be able to feel the ribs easily and see a waist from above. Weigh treats into the daily ration, use part of each meal for training, and choose a food with clear feeding guidance — our guide to the best dachshund dog food covers how to choose and portion food for this breed.

    Other Everyday Health Issues to Watch

    Not every concern is dramatic. A few smaller issues are worth building into your routine:

    • Teeth. Small dogs have crowded mouths, and dental disease is common in dogs generally. Daily brushing with a dog toothpaste and a dental check at every vet visit are the simplest protection. Bad breath is not normal.
    • Cold sensitivity. VCA describes smooth-coated dachshunds as sensitive to cold because they are small and short-coated. A coat for winter walks is comfort, not vanity.
    • Ears, nails and skin. Long ears trap moisture and long nails change how a dog stands, adding strain to the back. Our coat-by-coat guide on how to groom a dachshund covers nail trims and ear checks.

    A Back-Friendly Care Checklist

    1. Ask for test results before you buy. For Miniature Wirehaired puppies ask for Lafora results; for Miniature Longhaired, cord1 PRA. Ask whether the parents were IVDD X-ray screened.
    2. Weigh your dog monthly. Write it down — small gains are easy to miss on a small dog.
    3. Use ramps or lift your dog on and off furniture and in and out of the car. The PDSA advises avoiding chasing, jumping and stairs for dogs at risk.
    4. Keep exercise steady, not explosive. Regular walks build muscle that supports the spine; our page on dachshund exercise requirements shows how much is enough.
    5. Lift correctly. One hand under the chest, one under the hindquarters, keeping the back level.
    6. Use a harness, not a collar, for dogs that pull.
    7. Brush teeth daily and book a yearly health check that includes the mouth and eyes.
    8. Know your emergency plan. Save your vet’s and nearest emergency clinic’s numbers, and keep a crate ready.

    Common Mistakes Loving Owners Make

    • Waiting to see if it passes. Back pain that “seems better tomorrow” can worsen quickly. Mild signs still deserve a vet visit within 24 hours.
    • Letting a recovering dog run free too soon. Weeks of strict rest feel cruel, but they are what lets the disc heal.
    • Showing love with food. A few extra treats a day can mean a noticeably heavier dachshund within months.
    • Buying on looks alone. Rare colors and tiny sizes do not come with health testing — ask for paperwork, not promises.
    • Assuming a wirehaired or longhaired dog is safe from IVDD. Risk is lower in some coats, not zero.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the most common health problem in dachshunds?

    Intervertebral disc disease (IVDD). Dachshund Health UK estimates that about 1 in 4 dachshunds will have some degree of back disease during their lifetime, most often between the ages of 4 and 8.

    At what age do dachshunds usually get IVDD?

    IVDD is a particular risk between 4 and 7 years of age, and in the UK breed survey 76% of affected dogs were diagnosed between 4 and 8. Younger and older dogs can still be affected.

    Can a dachshund recover from IVDD without surgery?

    Often, yes. For grade 1 and 2 cases, conservative care with strict rest and pain relief has reported recovery rates of 55–100%. Dogs that cannot walk usually have better odds with surgery, so a fast vet assessment matters.

    Which dachshund health tests should I ask a breeder for?

    Ask for Lafora disease DNA results in Miniature Wirehaired dachshunds, cord1 PRA DNA results in Miniature Longhaired dachshunds, and, where available, IVDD X-ray screening scores for the parents.

    How long do dachshunds live?

    VCA Animal Hospitals gives a typical lifespan of 12–14 years. Lean body weight, back-friendly habits and early vet care help a dachshund reach the upper end of that range.

    Is it an emergency if my dachshund suddenly can’t walk?

    Yes. Paralysis or rapidly worsening wobbliness should be treated as an emergency — call your vet or an emergency clinic straight away and keep your dog still in a crate on the way.

    Caring for Your Dachshund for the Long Run

    Most dachshund common health problems are either preventable or far more treatable when caught early. Keep your dog lean, cut down on jumping, ask breeders for DNA and screening results, and never wait out back pain. Those habits cost little and protect the dog who greets you at the door every day.
