Dachshund Grooming

How to Groom a Wirehaired Dachshund by Coat Texture: Pin Wire, Harsh Wire and Silky Wire

Back to latest

In This Article

Jump straight to the section you need.

    To groom a wirehaired Dachshund well, sort the coat by texture first: a pin wire, a standard harsh wire, a soft wire and a silky wire do not want the same brush, schedule or finish. One dog may need little more than a stiff brush, while a littermate grows a fluffy beard and knots behind the elbows.

    Quick answer

    Pin wire: brush, and expect little or no stripping. Harsh wire: brush a few times a week and hand-strip the dead top coat a few times a year. Soft or silky wire: comb more often, test whether hair pulls out easily, and ask a groomer for a tidy trim if it does not.

    What are the wirehaired Dachshund coat types?

    The correct wire coat is a short, harsh topcoat over an undercoat. Owners and breeders also talk about pin wire (very short and hard), soft wire (longer and often curly) and silky wire (finer, with a longhaired influence).

    The reference point is the FCI Dachshund standard. It describes an even, close-fitting, dense wiry topcoat with undercoat everywhere except the muzzle, eyebrows and ears, plus a clearly defined beard, bushy eyebrows and very short hair on the ears. It calls soft hair on the top of the head and on the paws highly undesirable, and lists soft, curly, wavy or stand-off coats among the faults for the wirehaired variety. Those are show criteria. A soft-coated pet is not a lesser companion; it simply needs a different routine.

    Pin wire

    Sunsong Dachshunds describes a pin wire coat as a correct harsh double coat that is shorter, with less beard and leg hair than a usual wire coat, and often little or no face furnishing. From across the room it can pass for a smooth Dachshund. Run your fingers against the lie of the hair and you should feel a firm outer layer with a denser, softer layer beneath.

    Standard harsh wire

    This is the coat most people picture: a rough jacket, a definite beard and eyebrows, and wiry hair on the legs. The legs and face tell you more than the back does. Sunsong makes the same point, noting that leg hair that feels soft is a giveaway even when the back feels convincingly rough.

    Soft wire and silky wire

    The breeder SK Dox separates these two in its Dachshund coat notes. It uses soft wire for an improper wire coat that runs longer and curlier, and silky wire for the softer, finer result of the wire trait on a longhaired base. Both are still wirehaired dogs. You may hear the words swapped in conversation, so describe what your hands find rather than relying on the label from a sales listing.

    Pin wire vs harsh wire vs soft and silky wire: how do they compare?

    Pin and harsh wires have a firm outer coat; soft and silky wires do not, and that single difference drives the choice between brushing, stripping and trimming. Use this table after you have checked legs, back and face.

    CoatFeelLookMain home jobDead-coat method

    Harsh wireRough, including the legsClear beard and eyebrowsBrush and comb a few times a weekHand-strip when the top coat is ready

    The last two rows are practical suggestions built on how the coat behaves, not a rule from a kennel club. Your own dog can sit between rows, which is why the sorter has a mixed result.

    Why littermates can differ

    Coat type is inherited, and the combinations matter. A genome study of more than 1,000 dogs from 80 breeds found that variants in three genes account for most coat phenotypes in purebred dogs. One of them, RSPO2, is tied to furnishings such as moustache and eyebrows, and wirehaired Dachshunds were part of that analysis. Another, FGF5, relates to length. The authors list long, soft hair with furnishings as one of the possible combinations, which helps explain why a bearded dog need not have a hard coat. SK Dox adds that even two ideal wire parents can produce puppies with a range of coats. For how pigment fits into the picture, see Dachshund colors and markings.

    How can you test your Dachshund's coat texture at home?

    Check four places on a clean, dry, brushed dog: the legs, the back, the head and the paws. Then see whether a few topcoat hairs release easily when gently pulled in the direction of growth.

    1. Brush first. Loose undercoat and dirt make any coat feel softer or rougher than it is.
    2. Start on the legs. Roll a small pinch between finger and thumb. A proper wire feels coarse; a soft coat folds and clings.
    3. Part the back. Raise a fold of skin and look for long outer hairs standing away from a woolly layer. That shows you the two coats.
    4. Compare head and paws. A soft topknot or soft feet points toward a softer coat even when the saddle is rough.
    5. Try a tiny release test. Take only a few long outer hairs and draw them in the direction they grow. If they are ready, they come away without fuss. If they hold or your dog objects, stop.
    6. Write it down. Note the date and each zone. Repeat in a few weeks, because a blown, overdue coat can feel different from a freshly stripped one.

    Puppies are harder to read than adults, since the adult jacket and beard are still arriving. Build handling habits early with the Dachshund puppy care guide and postpone big coat decisions until you can see what actually grows.

    How should you brush each wire coat?

    Brush often enough that dead hair and knots never build up: a few sessions a week suits most harsh wires, with extra combing for softer coats. Always finish by checking with a comb.

    The Dachshund Club of Queensland suggests brushing wirehairs a few times a week, using a slicker brush before any stripping knife. Sunsong recommends two to three sessions with a bristle brush and a wide-toothed metal comb.

    Pin wire routine

    Use a stiff or bristle brush with short strokes in the direction of growth. Go over the chest and the backs of the legs, then run your hand over the whole dog. There is little beard to manage, so five unhurried minutes often covers it. Resist adding sharp de-shedding blades just because the coat looks too simple to need them.

    Harsh wire routine

    Brush the body, then comb the beard, eyebrows and leg hair separately. The beard collects food and water, so wipe it after meals and comb it once dry. Check the armpits and the area where a harness sits, since rubbing creates small tangles there. If you are choosing a harness around that thick chest, our Dachshund harness and coat size chart helps with measurements.

    Soft and silky wire routine

    Work in sections. Hold the hair near the skin with one hand and comb the ends first, then move toward the root so you do not drag on the skin. Ears, elbows, chest, belly, the backs of the thighs and the tail deserve their own pass. A slicker used lightly can help, but a comb is what proves a section is clear. If a mat sits tight to the skin, let a groomer remove it; scissors near a hidden skin fold are risky.

    When should you hand-strip, and when is trimming better?

    Hand-strip a firm wire coat when the dead top layer looks dull and releases easily. Choose a groomer's trim when hair holds tightly, the coat is silky, or your dog will not tolerate plucking.

    Published schedules vary. The Queensland club says wirehairs with thick undercoats need stripping three or four times a year and pin wires not at all. Sunsong puts it at two or three times a year, and SK Dox says its pet wires get by with twice a year using an undercoat rake. Treat these as a range and let the coat decide the date: a dull, lifted, shaggy jacket is a better signal than the calendar.

    Hand-stripping step by step

    1. Pick a calm moment after exercise, with your dog on a non-slip surface.
    2. Brush thoroughly. Do not bathe immediately beforehand; clean, slippery hair is harder to grip.
    3. Start at the neck. Support the skin with one hand.
    4. With the other, take a few long hairs between finger and thumb and pull them out in the direction of growth. Pull, do not twist or cut.
    5. Work in small patches down one side, then the other, then the back.
    6. Stop while your dog is still relaxed. Several sessions over a few days are normal.

    A stripping knife can help you grip. The club's method is to hold a small section against the knife with your thumb and draw it toward you, pulling instead of slicing. Stop if skin looks red or sore, or if your dog flinches repeatedly at one place.

    What clipping changes

    Clipping cuts hair instead of removing the dead shaft. Groomarts, a grooming training provider, warns that clipped wiry coats can turn curly, frizzy and faded, and the Queensland club advises against shaving any Dachshund. That is a reason to keep clippers off a good harsh coat. For a soft or silky coat that never strips cleanly, a conservative trim that leaves length may still be the kinder, more realistic choice. Tell the groomer you want tidying, not a shave, and ask what the regrowth may look like.

    How often should a wirehaired Dachshund have a bath?

    Bathe when your dog is dirty or smelly rather than by habit. Sources range from about once a month to every couple of months for wires.

    The Queensland club mentions roughly monthly bathing, while Sunsong suggests every couple of months. Use a dog shampoo, rinse until the water runs clear, towel well and comb again as the coat dries. Frequent washing and conditioners can leave a harsh jacket feeling softer for a while, so do not judge texture on bath day. A muddy belly after a wet walk can usually be rinsed and towelled without a full wash.

    Beard, eyebrows, feet and ears

    Keep the furnishings that make the face, and trim only what blocks sight or collects mess. Use blunt-nosed scissors, point them away from the eyes and cut a little at a time. Trim hair that grows past the paw pads so your dog has grip. Handle ears gently and keep scissors off the thin leather edges. For nails, work in tiny amounts and ask a groomer to show you the first time.

    A texture-based grooming checklist

    Pick one list for your dog and run it for a month before changing tools.

    • Record leg, back, head and paw texture.
    • Pin wire: stiff brush a few times a week; skip routine stripping.
    • Harsh wire: brush and comb 2–3 times a week; check for ready-to-strip topcoat each month.
    • Soft or silky wire: comb friction areas often; book a trim before knots spread.
    • Wipe and comb the beard; check feet after walks.
    • Photograph the coat after grooming so you can compare regrowth.
    • Give small rewards and end on an easy step.

    What to tell your groomer

    Bring your notes and say which zones feel harsh or soft, how often you brush, whether the coat has ever been clipped, and what finish you want. Ask whether they hand-strip, whether they can do it in more than one visit, and what they will do if the hair will not release. A good answer describes a test patch rather than a promise.

    Common mistakes with wirehaired coats

    • Choosing tools by the word “wire” alone. A silky wire raked like a hard terrier jacket can end up thin and uncomfortable.
    • Checking only the back. Legs, head and paws often reveal the softer side of a mixed coat.
    • Brushing the surface. Fluffy hair can look tidy over a mat. Part it and comb to the skin.
    • Plucking hair that holds. Force turns grooming into a fight. Wait, or switch method.
    • Doing the whole dog in one sitting. Short sessions protect patience and your dog's long back from awkward holding.
    • Shaving for convenience. Regrowth may not match what you had.

    Frequently asked questions about wirehaired Dachshund grooming

    Does a pin wire Dachshund need hand-stripping?

    Usually not. Both the Dachshund Club of Queensland and Sunsong Dachshunds describe pin wire coats as needing little or no stripping, just regular brushing and tidying.

    Is a silky wire Dachshund still wirehaired?

    Yes. SK Dox describes silky wires as wirehairs whose coat does not match the show standard, with more grooming and trimming around the eyes and feet.

    Can I clip a soft-coated wirehaired Dachshund?

    A groomer can discuss a trim for a coat that will not strip. Understand that clipping can change texture and colour, and avoid shaving close to the skin.

    How often should a harsh wire coat be stripped?

    Published advice ranges from two to four times a year. Let dull, easily released topcoat set the timing for your own dog.

    Why does my Dachshund have almost no beard?

    Pin wires can have little or no facial furnishing while still having a harsh double coat. Check the legs and body texture before deciding which coat you have.

    Will a puppy's soft coat turn harsh?

    Do not count on any particular result from a puppy feel. Brush gently, keep notes and reassess when the adult jacket and furnishings have come in.

    Groom the coat your hands find

    Good wirehaired Dachshund grooming starts with a two-minute check of legs, back, head and paws. Brush a pin wire, brush and strip a harsh wire, and comb and trim a softer coat. When zones disagree, treat them separately and let a groomer try a small patch first.
