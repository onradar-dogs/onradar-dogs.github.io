Best Training for Stubborn Dachshunds: Your Tribe’s Guide

Clear cues, humane techniques, and room for your dog’s whole personality.

Wondering whether the best training for stubborn Dachshunds means becoming more forceful? Start by making the next lesson easier to understand and worthwhile for your dog. You can appreciate your little hound’s personality, acknowledge your own frustration, and still build reliable household habits.

What is the best training for a stubborn Dachshund?

Choose reward-based training: show your dog what to do, recognize the successful response, and provide something they value. Manage the environment so mistakes are less likely. The AVSAB humane training statement recommends reward-based methods for everyday skills and unwanted behaviors, and advises against methods relying on pain, force, or discomfort.

That gives our Dachshund tribe a practical starting point: a clear request, an achievable task, and a reward. Being kind does not require leaving the front door open or allowing access to every room. Put a gate in place while you teach a safer greeting. Keep the same house rule even when the lesson needs to become easier.

Why does a Dachshund seem stubborn?

Independence and competing interests matter. AKC’s Dachshund breed guidance describes an intelligent, independent dog with a strong prey drive that responds best to positive, reward-based training. A fascinating smell or moving animal can compete with your request.

Before calling a missed cue defiance, ask what happened immediately before it. Was the dog successful in the kitchen but distracted at the park? Did you ask for a behavior you have barely practiced? Did the reward seem uninteresting? Those observations tell you what to change; a personality label does not.

Your dog can be affectionate and independent. You can love your dog and need a break. Make room for both feelings. Write “looked toward the hedge when called” in your notes rather than “was bad again.” For background, our Dachshund temperament guide belongs alongside practical observation, not in place of it.

A treat is not the only possible reward. AVSAB explains how preferred activities can reinforce other behaviors. Try a brief check-in followed by a release to sniff; record whether your dog becomes more willing to check in. Avoid making access to drinking water or rest contingent on performing a cue.

How can you teach attention and recall without a battle?

Start where your dog can succeed

Choose a quiet room. Keep a reward ready, say your dog’s name, and recognize a turn toward you. If nothing happens, pause and simplify: move closer or wait for a voluntary glance. End with a response you can reward instead of repeating the name louder.

Build an easy return

Practice a cheerful recall over a short distance indoors. Reward arrival, then allow another comfortable activity. For outdoor practice, use secure containment and a suitable leash or long line; choose a clear area and avoid letting the line snag. This is a practice setup, not evidence that your dog is ready for off-leash access.

AKC’s explanation of missed cues emphasizes teaching around distractions gradually and choosing rewards from the dog’s perspective. It also identifies frustration, fear, and anxiety as reasons performance may change. If the garden is harder than the living room, go back to an easier location and build from there.

How do you fit training into an ordinary day?

Pick an existing moment: clipping on the leash, preparing a meal, or settling after a walk. Choose a single goal for that moment. Ask everyone in the household to use the same cue and reward the same response; write the agreement somewhere visible.

AKC’s Dachshund puppy training guide recommends limiting puppy sessions to five minutes, a regular housetraining schedule with praise, and avoiding jumps off furniture because of disc disease concerns. A floor-level mat is a sensible place for this article’s exercises. Stop sooner when your puppy is no longer comfortable or engaged.

For accidents, record the opportunity you missed and revise supervision or the outing routine. For a missed recall, record the distraction. Keep daily movement appropriate to your individual dog; our Dachshund exercise guide can help frame questions for your veterinarian. New owners can also use the Dachshund puppy care guide while organizing their routine.

When should the tribe ask for professional help?

For fear, anxiety, or aggression, use a veterinarian and a qualified professional who explains reward-based methods. AVSAB describes treatment as potentially including environmental management, behavior modification, and medication when appropriate. Meanwhile, avoid situations that provoke a reaction and protect people and animals with distance and barriers.

Pause training and contact your veterinarian about a sudden change or apparent discomfort. Do not force a sit, manipulate your dog into position, or use this planner as a health assessment.

Ask a prospective trainer what they will do when your dog does not respond, how they will measure progress, and whether you may observe a session. Choose support that respects your household’s limits and your dog’s comfort. You do not have to prove you can solve every problem alone.

What do owners ask about training stubborn Dachshunds?

What is the best training for stubborn Dachshunds?

Use reward-based training, clear cues, and practice in an easy setting. Reward the behavior you want and adjust the task when your dog struggles.

Should I repeat a cue when my Dachshund ignores it?

Avoid repeating it in a loop. Pause, reduce the distraction, and practice an easier version before asking again.

How long should a Dachshund puppy training session last?

AKC recommends limiting Dachshund puppy sessions to five minutes. Stop sooner if your puppy loses interest or becomes uncomfortable.

Can sniffing be a training reward?

Yes, a preferred activity can reinforce another behavior. After an easy check-in on a safe walk, you can release your dog to sniff.

Does reward-based training mean having no boundaries?

No. Use gates, supervision, and a predictable routine to manage access, while teaching and rewarding the behavior you want.

When should I get professional help?

Seek veterinary and qualified behavior help for fear, anxiety, aggression, or a sudden behavior change. Keep everyone safe and avoid practicing situations that provoke a reaction.

The best training for stubborn Dachshunds gives you a repeatable next step: make the task achievable, reward the response, and adjust using what you observe. Your tribe can celebrate a small success without demanding a different personality from either end of the leash.

How do you make dachshund training feel less like a battle?

A stubborn dachshund often looks like a dog refusing to listen, but the useful training question is more specific: what is the dog getting from the current choice, and what easier reward can you offer for the choice you want? A dachshund that ignores a recall in the yard may be choosing scent, movement, or distance. A dachshund that freezes at the door may be avoiding weather, noise, stairs, or pressure. Treating every hesitation as defiance makes the plan too emotional. Treating it as information gives you something to adjust.

Start by lowering the difficulty before you raise your voice. Move closer, use a shorter cue, reduce distractions, and reward the first small movement in the right direction. If the dog cannot succeed three times in a row, the setup is probably too hard. That is not a character flaw in the dog or the owner. It means the training step needs to be smaller. Dachshunds often do better when the task feels like a quick bargain: one clear cue, one easy action, one meaningful reward, then a break.

Keep sessions short enough that the dog finishes wanting more. Five clean repetitions are more valuable than twenty frustrated ones. End before the dog starts sniffing away, barking, or offering random behaviors. A consistent stopping point also helps the owner stay calm. The goal is not to win an argument with a small dog. The goal is to build a pattern where listening pays better than ignoring you.

What does a realistic two-week reset look like?

For the first three days, pick one cue only. Many owners try to fix recall, leash pulling, barking, door manners, and handling at the same time. That makes progress hard to see. Choose the cue that matters most for safety or daily peace, then practice it in the easiest possible setting. If the target is recall, start indoors. If the target is “leave it,” start with a boring object. If the target is calm handling, start with one-second touches instead of a full grooming session.

Days four through seven are for adding one mild distraction. Do not jump from the living room to a busy sidewalk. Add a toy on the floor, another person in the room, or a quieter part of the yard. Reward the same behavior generously. If the dog fails repeatedly, return to the easier step. That return is not going backward; it is how you protect the cue from becoming background noise.

During the second week, practice the cue in two real-life moments. For example, ask for the recall before opening the garden door, or ask for a calm pause before clipping the leash. Keep the reward practical: food, access to sniffing, permission to move forward, or a favorite game. Dachshunds are often motivated by outcomes, not praise alone. When the dog learns that cooperation opens the next good thing, training becomes less about obedience theater and more about daily communication.

Which owner habits accidentally make stubborn behavior stronger?

The first habit is repeating the cue until it becomes meaningless. If “come” is said ten times while the dog keeps sniffing, the dog learns that the first nine versions do not matter. Say the cue once, make the next step easier, and reward movement toward you. If you cannot make success likely, do not spend the cue in that situation yet.

The second habit is rewarding the final moment after a long negotiation. If the dog ignores you for two minutes and then receives the same reward as an immediate response, the delay becomes part of the routine. Instead, practice in a simpler setting where fast responses are possible. Save bigger rewards for the cleanest attempts. Calm timing matters more than dramatic correction.

The third habit is forgetting the dachshund's body. Long-backed dogs may hesitate around stairs, jumping, slippery floors, or rough handling. If a dog suddenly refuses a behavior it used to know, consider comfort and context before assuming attitude. A training plan should make the wanted behavior clear, but it should also respect the dog's physical limits and the owner's responsibility to keep practice safe.

How should you track whether the plan is working?

Use a simple scorecard for one cue. Write the date, location, distraction level, reward used, and whether the dog responded on the first cue. Do not write a long diary. You only need enough detail to see whether the same setup is getting easier. If first-cue responses rise over a week, the plan is working. If they do not, change the reward, reduce the distraction, or split the behavior into a smaller step.

Also track owner consistency. Did everyone use the same cue? Did anyone repeat it five times? Did the dog get rewarded for the behavior you actually wanted? Many “stubborn dog” problems are partly human timing problems. That is good news, because timing can improve quickly once the household agrees on the rule.

After two weeks, keep what works and drop what does not. A good dachshund training plan should feel calmer, clearer, and more repeatable. If the dog is still escalating, guarding, biting, panicking, or refusing because of suspected pain, bring in a qualified trainer or veterinarian. Stubbornness is a training label; distress, pain, and unsafe behavior deserve a professional plan.
