How to Groom a Dachshund — The Complete Guide for Dachshund People

Wondering how to groom a Dachshund without pulling tangled hair or turning nail care into a wrestling match? Start with your dog's coat type, then build a routine around comfortable handling, clean skin, and small tasks your dog can tolerate. This guide separates everyday home care from jobs that need a groomer or veterinarian.

Updated October 7, 2026. Informational guidance for owners; dogs with skin disease, ear pain, or a history of back problems need an individual veterinary care plan.

What does your Dachshund's coat actually need?

A smooth Dachshund needs gentle removal of loose hair; a longhaired dog also needs careful detangling; a wirehaired dog may need specialist coat maintenance. The American Kennel Club's Dachshund profile distinguishes these three varieties. Choose tools for the coat in front of you rather than assuming every Dachshund needs the same haircut.

How do you set up a safe grooming space?

Use a nonslip surface, keep supplies within reach, and arrange the session so your dog cannot jump from a counter or table. Floor-level brushing on a washable mat is often easier than managing an elevated station. A towel alone may slide on tile, so check that the surface stays steady when your dog shifts weight.

For lifting, support both the front and rear of the body rather than letting the hindquarters hang. Keep the dog close to you and avoid twisting while holding them. A dog recovering from spinal disease may need specific restrictions; follow the treating veterinarian's instructions. Our guide to IVDD in Dachshunds provides background for that conversation.

Prepare the brush, comb, dog shampoo, towels, dog nail clippers or file, and small rewards before beginning. Keep electrical tools away from water. Do not leave a dog unattended in a tub or on a grooming table, even briefly to fetch something.

How should you brush each coat type?

Smooth coats: light pressure, short sessions

Work with the direction of hair growth using a soft brush or rubber mitt. Focus on removing loose hair rather than repeatedly scrubbing one patch. Check the chest, belly, and armpits gently: thinly covered skin does not need vigorous brushing. Finish by running your hands over the coat to look for an unusual lump, scab, or sore area.

A smooth coat does not need routine clipping. If the brush makes the skin red, or your dog repeatedly flinches at one spot, stop and inspect rather than applying more pressure. Increased scratching, bald patches, or damaged skin need a different response from ordinary shedding.

Longhaired coats: check underneath the pretty surface

Brush small sections, then use a comb to check whether the hair is clear through its depth. The coat can look tidy on top while hiding knots behind the ears, in the armpits, under the chest, and around the hind legs. Support the hair near its base while working on a loose tangle so the brush does not tug directly on skin.

Start at the ends of a small loose knot and work gradually inward. Keep sessions comfortable and use a dog-safe detangling product only as directed. If a comb catches repeatedly, do not drag it through. Tight mats close to skin need a groomer's assessment; pulling harder can hurt, and cutting blindly with scissors risks cutting the skin folded into the mat.

The AKC guide to brush types explains why different tools suit different coats. A brush that worked on a friend's dog is not automatically suitable for yours. Avoid scraping the skin with firm slicker strokes or repeatedly using an aggressive shedding tool.

Wirehaired coats: separate brushing from stripping

Comb through the coat and clean food or debris from the beard. Hand-stripping is a skilled process for maintaining a suitable wire coat by removing ready-to-release hairs; it is not a reason to pull random hairs at home. Ask a groomer experienced with wirehaired Dachshunds whether your dog's coat, skin, and tolerance make stripping appropriate.

Clipping can be a practical choice for some pet dogs, but discuss how it may affect the coat's appearance and texture. Comfort takes priority over achieving a show finish. A senior dog or one with health problems should not endure a long session simply to meet an aesthetic preference.

When and how should you bathe a Dachshund?

Bathe when the coat is dirty or your veterinarian's treatment plan calls for it. There is no universal six-week rule for every dog. Spot-cleaning paws and belly after a walk can handle local dirt without repeatedly washing the whole coat. Persistent odor despite ordinary cleaning deserves investigation rather than stronger perfume.

  1. Inspect first. Identify sore skin and tangles before adding water. If the dog has tight mats, get advice before bathing.
  2. Make footing secure. Use a nonslip tub mat and lukewarm water. Introduce the spray gently, away from the face.
  3. Use dog shampoo. Follow dilution and contact-time instructions. A medicated shampoo should be used according to veterinary directions, not selected because the label sounds reassuring.
  4. Wash carefully. Work over the body without rubbing painful areas. Clean the face with a damp cloth and avoid sending water or shampoo into eyes and ears.
  5. Rinse thoroughly. Check the belly, armpits, and folds where residue can remain.
  6. Dry and settle. Blot with towels, dry damp friction areas, and keep the dog warm. If using a dryer, use a low, cool setting, keep it moving, and stop if the dog becomes distressed.

Do not treat a damp dog as ready for rough play on a slippery floor. Have the resting area prepared before lifting them out of the bath. The AKC's coat-maintenance guidance is a useful general reference alongside instructions specific to your dog's products and health.

How can you trim nails without guessing?

Inspect every nail, including dewclaws if present. The quick contains sensitive tissue and blood vessels; cutting into it is painful. Clear nails may make it easier to see, while dark nails require extra caution. Ask a veterinarian or qualified groomer to demonstrate technique on your dog if you are unsure.

Use dog nail clippers that fit the nail or a suitable file. Take only small amounts from the tip and keep the paw in a comfortable position. There is no requirement to finish all four feet in one sitting. One calm nail today can be a better training result than a full trim performed under force.

The PDSA's veterinary nail-trimming advice emphasizes avoiding the quick, checking dewclaws, and getting help when needed. If bleeding occurs, stop, apply gentle pressure with clean gauze, and contact your veterinarian for advice. Painful, split, or ingrown nails should be assessed professionally.

What ear and dental care belongs in the routine?

Lift each ear flap gently and look for redness, discharge, or a change in smell. A clean, comfortable ear does not automatically need cleaning because another week has passed. Ask your veterinarian which cleaner and frequency are appropriate, especially if your dog has had infections.

When cleaning has been recommended, follow the product and veterinary instructions, then wipe accessible debris gently. Do not push cotton swabs into the canal or use homemade alcohol or peroxide mixtures. If the ear is painful or inflamed, seek advice before cleaning. VCA's ear-care guidance explains both the risks of over-cleaning and situations needing veterinary assessment.

Dental care is a separate daily habit rather than something saved for bath day. Introduce a dog toothbrush and dog toothpaste gradually, beginning with brief comfortable contact. Never use human toothpaste. Brushing does not remove established hardened tartar or replace examination of a painful mouth; VCA's toothbrushing guidance explains home prevention. Bleeding gums, difficulty eating, or a loose tooth warrant veterinary advice.

How do you help a dog who dislikes grooming?

Break the task into steps the dog can accept: see the brush, receive a reward; feel one gentle stroke, receive a reward; take a break. For nails, first practice touching a shoulder, then briefly touching a paw, before introducing the tool. Increase difficulty only while the dog stays comfortable.

Pulling away, freezing, growling, or trying to escape are reasons to pause, not evidence that the dog needs firmer restraint. Sudden resistance can signal pain. Our advice on training Dachshunds with rewards is relevant to practicing these small cooperative steps.

For a puppy, prioritize calm introductions over a complete cosmetic groom. Our Dachshund puppy care guide can help place grooming within the wider routine. Severe fear or a bite risk calls for a veterinarian and an experienced behavior professional; do not improvise sedation at home.

What should you do at home, and what should you delegate?

Home care works best for manageable brushing, checking skin, washing ordinary dirt, and handling exercises. Delegate tight mat removal, unfamiliar nail work, and specialized coat finishing. Ask a groomer about experience with your coat variety, nonslip handling, breaks, and how they respond if the dog shows pain or distress.

For appointments, describe known spinal problems, skin treatments, and handling triggers in advance. Request the services actually needed rather than an automatic package: brushing and a nail assessment may be sufficient, while a longhaired dog with tangles needs more time. Costs vary by location, coat condition, and service, so obtain a specific quote instead of relying on an invented nationwide price.

Track recurring redness, lumps, unusual odor, and changes in tolerance. Our overview of common Dachshund health problems provides broader context, but new symptoms need a veterinary assessment. Grooming is an opportunity to notice changes early, not a way to diagnose them.

Frequently asked grooming questions

How often should I brush a Dachshund?

Start with weekly gentle brushing for a smooth coat, frequent tangle checks for a longhaired coat, and brushing or combing once or twice weekly for a wire coat. Adjust to dirt, tangles, and comfort rather than following the calendar alone.

Does a smooth Dachshund need a haircut?

Usually no. Gentle brushing and ordinary hygiene are generally enough for the coat. Ask a professional about any specific skin or hygiene issue before using clippers.

Should every wirehaired Dachshund be hand-stripped?

No single technique suits every pet. An experienced groomer should assess coat suitability, skin health, and tolerance, then discuss stripping or a practical trim.

Can I cut a tight mat out with scissors?

Avoid cutting tight mats close to skin yourself. Skin can be hidden inside a mat, making a seemingly simple cut dangerous. Ask a groomer to remove it safely.

Should I clean the ears after every bath?

Check the ears, but do not automatically apply cleaner. Follow your veterinarian's plan, and seek advice if there is pain, redness, discharge, or an unusual smell.

What if my dog will only tolerate a minute of grooming?

Use that minute for one easy task and finish calmly. Practice short rewarded handling sessions separately. Sudden or intense resistance deserves a pain assessment before further training.
